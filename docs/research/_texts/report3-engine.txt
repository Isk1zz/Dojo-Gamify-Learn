Empirical Audit and Psychometric
Architecture of Knell’s Learning Engine
Immediate Mastery Gating Versus Delayed Testing
Retrieval Strength Versus Storage Strength and the Fluency Illusion
The fundamental architectural vulnerability in the existing mastery gate is the temporal proximity
of the five-item summative examination to the initial instructional sequence. According to the
New Theory of Disuse formulated by Bjork and Bjork, human memory traces must be modeled
across two functionally independent dimensions: retrieval strength, which indexes the
immediate cognitive accessibility of a representation in the presence of proximal cues, and
storage strength, which measures how deeply entrenched, interconnected, and permanent that
representation is within long-term semantic networks. When an assessment is administered
immediately after an examinee navigates explanatory text, inspects worked examples, and
resolves formative quiz prompts within the same unbroken session, retrieval strength operates
at its absolute biological peak.
This operational state induces the fluency illusion, often identified in metacognitive literature as
the mismeasure of memory. Subjective ease of processing and high cue-availability create an
internal feeling of competence that correlates poorly with durable retention. When examined
immediately post-study, human learners demonstrate elevated retrieval accuracy even for
superficially processed material, because the representations remain active in working memory
or transient recency buffers. Consequently, passing an immediate five-item test at an eighty
percent threshold provides robust evidence of momentary accessibility, but zero psychometric
assurance that the underlying knowledge has achieved structural consolidation in long-term
storage. Across extensive empirical evaluations of the testing effect, the relationship between
immediate performance and long-term retention routinely reverses over time: instructional
configurations that maximize acquisition performance often produce rapid forgetting, whereas
conditions that introduce retrieval difficulty depress immediate scores while dramatically
accelerating long-term durability.
Premature Entry Failure Modes in Spaced Repetition Schedules
The engine gates progression by requiring an eighty percent score on an immediate
examination, immediately qualifying the topic for entry into textbook SuperMemo-2 (SM-2)
scheduling. The mathematical formulation of SM-2 was developed under the explicit assumption
that an item entering its initial repetition cycle possesses authentic, baseline memory durability.
Under the current system, an unencoded topic that clears the immediate gate through transient
retrieval strength enters the scheduling queue at an initial interval of one day, advancing to six
days upon a single subsequent successful rating.
Because storage strength was never established during the initial learning event, the probability
of recall across a twenty-four-hour boundary drops steeply below the typical nominal retention
target of eighty-five to ninety percent. When the learner encounters the topic’s flashcards on the
subsequent day, they face an acute retrieval lapse or succumb to hindsight bias during

self-assessment. If the learner rates the topic favorably on Day 1 based on lingering familiarity,
the subsequent leap to a six-day interval triggers catastrophic forgetting. The item predictably
lapses, resetting repetition counters to zero and collapsing the schedule back to an interval of
one day. By using immediate performance to justify spaced Repetition 1 entry, the engine
systematically admits unstable traces into a schedule calibrated for stable memories, inducing
unnecessary remediation cycles, user frustration, and algorithmic inefficiency.
Comparative Evaluation of Remediation Architectures
Resolving this gating failure requires balancing psychometric validity against user experience
friction. Multi-stage intraday acquisition ladders—such as the one-minute, ten-minute, and
one-day intervals common in desktop flashcard applications—are structurally incompatible with
mobile educational software designed around a once-daily asynchronous usage pattern.
Forcing micro-intervals requires either continuous client sessions or intrusive mobile
notifications, both of which degrade engagement and drive learner attrition. Conversely,
maintaining an immediate examination while artificially compressing the initial spaced interval to
twelve hours fails to solve the measurement problem: it still records an unvalidated cognitive
event as an authenticated mastery transition in persistent database storage.
The most effective, empirically grounded intervention is the twenty-four-hour provisional
verification gate. Under this framework, clearing an immediate end-of-topic examination does
not grant full mastery or spaced repetition status; instead, it marks the topic as provisional,
permitting curriculum navigation while withholding formal entry into the review queue and the
visual progression mechanics. Formal entry requires passing a brief, objective verification check
upon the learner's next daily session, enforcing an inter-session delay of at least eighteen to
twenty-four hours.

Self-Reported Recall Grading and Metacognitive
Calibration
Metacognitive Monitoring Breakdown in Self-Assessed Flashcards
The reliance on a four-level self-reported confidence scale (Difficult, Still learning, Has an idea,
Known) with the model answer displayed on-screen introduces massive measurement error into
the review scheduler. Cognitive and educational psychology literature demonstrates that
untrained learners are fundamentally uncalibrated when evaluating their own knowledge
structures. When an answer is revealed prior to or simultaneously with self-assessment,
learners fall victim to hindsight bias—the persistent cognitive conviction that an answer was
known all along—and creeping determinism.
The presence of the model answer provides perceptual and semantic scaffolding. Learners
process the displayed text with high fluency, mistake that ease of comprehension for antecedent
retrieval, and rate the card as known. In landmark investigations by Dunlosky and Rawson,
students self-scoring their recall of complex definitions exhibited profound overconfidence,
driven almost entirely by commission errors—instances where the generated answer was
factually incorrect or conceptually irrelevant. When inspectable model answers were present,
students awarded full or partial credit to completely erroneous definitions between thirty and fifty
percent of the time.
Similarly, Grimaldi and Karpicke investigated learner self-scoring against automated algorithmic
grading (QuickScore) during retrieval practice. While automated text processing accurately
detected conceptual inaccuracies, students demonstrated a persistent, systematic bias to grant
themselves credit for objectively incorrect responses. In Knell’s engine, where five of the eight
cards in a deck originate from multiple-choice exam questions presented without answer
options, this failure mode is magnified: recognition-level knowledge cannot be reliably
self-graded under open reveal without semantic contamination.

Anki’s Four-Button Model: Ecological Tolerance Versus Psychometric
Rigor
The four-button self-report interface popularized by Anki is widely deployed, but its viability in
practice stems from community-enforced behavioral workarounds rather than intrinsic
psychometric soundess. Naturalistic cohorts of medical students and competitive test-takers
succeed with Anki because they adopt strict informal heuristics. These power users deliberately
enforce the minimum information principle—restricting cards to atomic, single-fact cloze
deletions where the answer is binary and unambiguous—and apply ruthless grading discipline,
marking any hesitation or partial recall as an immediate failure (Again).
When an identical self-report mechanism is applied to complex, narrative, or multi-point
conceptual content without specialized user training, the system breaks down. Ordinary learners
do not apply severe self-punishment heuristics; instead, they interpret the interface leniently.
Crucially, Anki contains no native economy, visual progression trees, or social status tokens. In
an environment where ratings control social reputation and cosmetics, Anki's behavioral
compromise collapses entirely.
Verification Interventions for Self-Scored Decks
To secure scheduling integrity without introducing excessive authoring overhead, potential
verification mechanisms must be evaluated across cognitive efficacy, interface friction, and
implementation cost.
Requiring learners to commit to a subjective confidence rating before revealing the answer
provides minimal protection: while it reduces hindsight bias slightly, post-reveal evaluation
remains distorted by lenient self-grading. Forcing typed overt retrieval prior to reveal eliminates
the illusion of having retrieved content when nothing was generated. However, comparing typed
text to a model answer still forces the learner into a subjective comparison role unless natural
language scoring is integrated.

The most effective, operationally sound solution is the integration of idea-unit checklists.
Rawson and Dunlosky demonstrated that when complex model answers are decomposed into
two or three constituent idea units, and learners must check binary boxes indicating the explicit
presence of each sub-component in their retrieval attempt, overconfidence on commission
errors declines by more than sixty percent. Quality is then computed deterministically by the
system based on verified idea-unit counts rather than a holistic self-rating. For flashcards
derived from chunk quizzes, the engine can transition entirely to objective micro-testing, such as
short-answer matching or two-distractor multiple choice, completely bypassing self-evaluation.

Psychometric Architecture of Mastery Assessment
Test Length Versus Classification Consistency: The Subkoviak
Formulation

The proposal to expand the summative topic examination from five to ten questions directly
addresses an acute psychometric reliability issue. In criterion-referenced mastery measurement,
reliability is not appropriately evaluated via classical internal consistency coefficients like
Cronbach’s alpha, which depend on broad inter-individual variance. Instead, criterion-referenced
reliability is measured through classification consistency (P_0), defined as the probability that an
examinee would be classified into the same mastery status across independent administrations
of the same test or parallel test forms.
Using Michael J. Subkoviak’s single-administration classification consistency estimator for
mastery tests, the coefficient of agreement P_0 represents the average probability of consistent
classification across a population of N examinees. For a test of length n with a raw cut-score c,
the consistency probability for an individual examinee possessing true latent proficiency p_i is
formulated as:
P_{c(i)} = \left[ \sum_{x=c}^{n} \binom{n}{x} p_i^x (1 - p_i)^{n-x} \right]^2 + \left[ \sum_{x=0}^{c-1}
\binom{n}{x} p_i^x (1 - p_i)^{n-x} \right]^2 P_0 = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} P_{c(i)}
Under the built machine's current configuration, n = 5 items with an eighty percent cutoff (c = 4
correct responses). For an examinee whose true latent proficiency is p = 0.70—a marginal
non-master who falls below the eighty percent standard and should be routed to
remediation—the cumulative binomial probability of scoring four or more correct answers on a
single test administration is:
P(X \ge 4) = 5(0.70)^4(0.30)^1 + (0.70)^5 = 5(0.2401)(0.30) + 0.16807 = 0.36015 + 0.16807 =
0.52822
This non-master has a 52.8% probability of passing the gate on a single test administration.
Because Knell’s engine provides an immediate retry on failure, the cumulative probability of this
non-master falsely clearing the mastery gate across two successive attempts is:
P(\text{False Pass in 2 Attempts}) = 1 - (1 - 0.52822)^2 = 1 - (0.47178)^2 = 1 - 0.22258 \approx
0.77742 \quad (\mathbf{77.7\%})
A five-question mastery gate is psychometrically indefensible: more than three out of four
sub-threshold learners will clear an eighty percent threshold purely through binomial variance
and test retries.
Expanding the assessment to n = 10 items with an eighty percent threshold (c = 8 correct
responses) substantially improves decision accuracy. For the identical marginal candidate (p =
0.70), the single-attempt pass probability drops to:
P(X \ge 8) = \sum_{x=8}^{10} \binom{10}{x} (0.70)^x (0.30)^{10-x} = 45(0.70)^8(0.30)^2 +
10(0.70)^9(0.30)^1 + (0.70)^{10} \approx 0.38278
The two-attempt false pass rate decreases from 77.7% down to 61.9%, while Subkoviak’s
population agreement coefficient P_0 rises from an unacceptably noisy range of 0.68–0.72 up to
0.82–0.85. Psychometric literature indicates that lengthening mastery tests beyond ten to twelve
items yields diminishing returns, as measurement error curves flatten while cognitive fatigue and
assessment abandonment escalate. Doubling the assessment from five to ten items represents
an optimal balance between psychometric reliability and user experience.

Question Format Diversity: Recognition Versus Constructed
Response
The proposal to incorporate diverse question formats—specifically moving beyond uniform
four-option multiple-choice questions to include constructed-response prompts and multi-step
problem solving—is supported by cognitive science. Standard multiple-choice items evaluate
recognition memory, allowing examinees to employ superficial heuristics: eliminating
transparently improbable distractors, capitalizing on lexical cues, or confirming familiar
fragments of phrasing. Recognition-based tasks bypass the reconstructive cognitive processes
that define genuine competence.
Constructed-response formats, cued recall, and multi-step procedural tasks demand generative
retrieval. To resolve a multi-step task, an examinee must access underlying schemas, sequence
sub-goals, and apply structural rules without perceptual support. In comparative assessments
by Smith and Karpicke, practicing retrieval through short-answer and constructed formats
produced substantially greater delayed retention and broader inferential transfer than practice
restricted to multiple-choice recognition. Adding multi-step problem-solving items transforms the
exam from a test of superficial recognition into an authentic evaluation of procedural fluency.
The Psychometric Hazard of Within-Session Item Reuse
The app owner's suggestion to construct the ten-item exam by reusing questions directly from
the within-chunk quizzes minutes earlier in the session introduces a critical psychometric
confound. Reusing identical items violates the core construct validity of a summative mastery
gate.

When a learner encounters an identical multiple-choice question minutes after answering it in a
chunk quiz, they do not engage in the targeted retrieval of abstract conceptual principles.
Instead, they retrieve the episodic memory of having recently engaged with that specific
question and its correct option letter. Furthermore, having seen the feedback and explanation on
the previous quiz trial, the distractors lose their diagnostic power. The item measures
working-memory recency priming rather than consolidated knowledge. Moreover, because
within-session repetition occurs without a meaningful temporal spacing interval, the second
presentation represents massed exposure, yielding negligible gains in storage strength.
To maintain valid assessment standards, exam items must consist exclusively of isomorphic
variants. An isomorphic item preserves the identical underlying mathematical structure,
conceptual relationship, or logical schema while systematically altering surface features,
phrasing, context, or variable values. Isomorphic testing neutralizes episodic pattern recognition
and requires the learner to actively decontextualize the principle, providing a reliable measure of
true conceptual transfer.

Decay-on-Neglect, Loss Aversion, and the Abstinence Violation Effect
The engine's withering mechanic penalizes overdue topics by reducing the scheduling interval
by one day for every day overdue past a seven-day grace period, causing visual plant assets to
shrink. While intended to mirror the biological reality of memory decay, this mechanism is
behaviorally counterproductive. It triggers the Abstinence Violation Effect—colloquially known as
the "what-the-hell effect" in addiction and behavioral adherence research.
In behavioural economics, loss aversion indicates that individuals experience the psychological
pain of losing an earned asset roughly twice as intensely as the pleasure of acquiring an
equivalent gain. When a learner who has dedicated months of sustained effort to cultivate
Blossom trees encounters an unavoidable disruption—such as illness, professional travel, or
academic overload—exceeding the seven-day grace window, they return to find their progress
visually defaced. Under the Abstinence Violation Effect, a perceived break in an idealized
standard transforms a minor lapse into a perceived total failure. Confronting a damaged garden
generates negative affective valence, shame, and conditioned avoidance. Rather than
undertaking the demoralizing effort to repair shrunken assets, users abandon the platform
entirely. The engine removed streaks to prevent abandonment driven by broken daily tallies, but
inadvertently reproduced the identical failure mode in botanical form.
Industry Precedent: The Removal of Duolingo’s "Cracked Skills"
Knell’s withering mechanic directly mirrors Duolingo’s legacy Cracked Skills mechanic. Between
2019 and 2022, Duolingo operated a system where gold-mastered skills displayed visual cracks
when their underlying spaced repetition intervals expired, demanding remediation before
learners could progress comfortably.
The behavioral consequences across Duolingo’s massive user base were overwhelmingly
negative:
● Completionist paralysis emerged as a primary behavioral distortion, with dedicated
learners spending their entire daily study time repairing early introductory lessons rather
than engaging with new curricular units.
● Users returning after brief hiatuses faced extensive walls of fractured icons, transforming
re-entry into a punitive remediation chore that accelerated permanent churn.
● In response to widespread user friction and engagement decay, Duolingo completely
retired cracked skills in November 2022. They redesigned their architecture into a linear
path where spaced repetition is handled programmatically through background
interleaving, preserving historic visual mastery intact and eliminating visible decay.

Transitioning from Destructive Withering to Visual Dormancy
The engine must decouple cognitive review urgency from visual destruction. Overdue topics
should never wither, shrink, or lose developmental tiers; a Blossom must remain a Blossom
indefinitely. When an item becomes overdue, the visual asset should transition into a state of
seasonal dormancy, such as frost covering the foliage or flowers closing, signaling that the plant
requires water without destroying its earned status.
Mathematically, intervals must not decay in persistent storage simply because of chronological
neglect. Forgetting during disuse does not follow a linear subtraction of elapsed days; an item
with a sixty-day interval that is unreviewed for fifteen days does not possess an active durability
of forty-five days. Instead, the overdue card remains at its current state in the review queue.
When the learner eventually completes the review, the subsequent interval is computed
dynamically based on their empirical performance on that trial. Finally, daily reputation
allowances must be completely decoupled from static garden weights and calculated exclusively
from active review volume and verification accuracy completed during the current session,
removing any economic incentive to manipulate flashcard ratings.
Micro-Instructional Phasing and Cognitive Boundaries
Pretesting Effects Across Sequential Chunk Boundaries
The authoring discrepancy documented in Knell's engine—where predict was restricted to the
initial chunk of a topic while authors spontaneously integrated recall across ninety-seven of one
hundred seventy-one chunks—stems from a misunderstanding of the cognitive boundaries
governing pretesting. The brief questions whether the pretesting effect requires an unstudied
topic state, or whether it functions effectively when positioned at the beginning of every chunk
within an ongoing topic.
Cognitive psychology establishes that the pretesting effect—learning facilitated through
unsuccessful, errorful generation prior to study—does not depend on macroscopic topical
novelty or general epistemic curiosity. In empirical studies comparing item-level and set-level
pretesting methodologies, Hollins et al. demonstrated that guessing an answer to an unfamiliar
prompt before instruction enhances memory exclusively for the specific target information
queried; it confers zero incidental memory boost to adjacent facts within the same explanatory
passage. The underlying cognitive mechanism operates via target-specific semantic activation
and prediction error generation.
When an examinee generates an incorrect guess, the subsequent exposure to instructional text
serves as corrective feedback. The neurocognitive mismatch between the predicted answer and
the correct solution triggers error-driven encoding, substantially potentiating long-term retention.
Because this mechanism is mediated by item-specific prediction errors rather than broad
situational novelty, the pretesting effect functions with equal potency at the head of every
individual chunk. Requiring a prediction at the opening of Chunk 2 or Chunk 3 primes the
cognitive system for the distinct relational concepts introduced in that sub-unit, completely
independent of whether the learner spent the preceding ten minutes working through Chunk 1.
Interim Testing and the Forward Testing Effect

Restricting free recall to the closing chunk of a topic directly violates findings on proactive
interference and the forward testing effect. Szpunar, McDermott, and Roediger established that
interpolating retrieval practice between sequential learning segments produces profound
cognitive benefits for ongoing encoding.
When learners study sequential text blocks without intermediate retrieval, memory traces from
earlier segments accumulate, generating severe proactive interference that impairs the
encoding and retention of subsequent material. Requiring an active retrieval prompt at the end
of each instructional chunk establishes an explicit event boundary, resetting working memory
resources and shielding subsequent chunks from proactive interference. Furthermore, interim
retrieval enhances sustained attention, reduces mind-wandering, and increases the cognitive
depth with which learners process subsequent instructional text.
The content authors who placed recall prompts across sixty-five non-terminal chunks were
acting in alignment with optimal cognitive architecture. Forcing free recall exclusively at the topic
boundary overburdens the final prompt, requiring the examinee to retrieve multiple disparate
concepts simultaneously and increasing retrieval failure. The instructional specification must be
updated to mandate both predict and recall across all one hundred seventy-one chunks,
creating a unified, five-phase pedagogical cycle throughout the platform.

Scheduler Dynamics, Dual-Purpose Scoring, and
Diagnostic Attribution

The Psychometric Hazard of Dual-Purpose Scoring
In Knell's engine, a single exam score performs two incompatible measurement roles: it
establishes a categorical mastery gate (Score \ge 80\%) and calculates the initial continuous
scheduling quality (\text{Quality} = \text{round}(Score / 20)). This dual assignment represents an
architectural anti-pattern. An immediate post-study score reflects transient retrieval fluency
under maximal cueing, not the rate of underlying memory decay.
In practice, an examinee who achieves eighty percent (four out of five items) enters the SM-2
algorithm with a quality score of 4, while an examinee who scores one hundred percent (five out
of five items) enters with a quality score of 5. Under textbook SM-2 ease dynamics:
\Delta EF = 0.1 - (5 - q) \cdot [0.08 + (5 - q) \cdot 0.02]
For q = 5, \Delta EF = +0.10, raising the ease factor from 2.50 to 2.60. For q = 4, \Delta EF =
0.00, leaving the ease factor unchanged at 2.50.
By the fifth repetition, these two learners will be assigned review intervals that diverge by
several weeks. Because a five-item test carries a standard error of measurement of
approximately 0.63 items, the distinction between four and five correct answers is largely
psychometric noise. Gating mastery and initializing interval dynamics from the same immediate
score magnifies measurement error and distorts scheduling intervals. The mastery gate must be
completely decoupled from scheduling parameters: the gate serves as a binary operational
threshold, while all items entering the review queue initialize with a neutral baseline interval (I_1
= 1\text{ day}, EF = 2.50).
The SM-2 Flat-Ease Anomaly at Quality 4
The engine's brief noted that at the pass mark (q = 4), the SM-2 ease adjustment formula
evaluates to zero, leaving the ease factor unchanged at 2.50. The team assumed this was
benign. It is not benign; in applied psychometrics, it represents interval explosion under
marginal performance.
In SM-2, intervals compound across repetitions:
● Repetition 1: I([span_138](start_span)[span_138](end_span)1) = 1\text{ day}
● Repetition 2: I(2) = 6\text{ days}
● Repetition 3: I(3) = \text{round}(6 \times 2.50) = 15\text{ days}
● Repetition 4: I(4) = \text{round}(15 \times 2.50) = 38\text{ days}
● Repetition 5: I(5) = \text{round}(38 \times 2.50) = 95\text{ days}
If an examinee consistently scrapes through reviews at a quality score of 4, they are signaling
significant retrieval effort, hesitation, and near-failure. In modern Bayesian cognitive
architectures—such as the Free Spaced Repetition Scheduler (FSRS) or the ACT-R declarative
memory module—marginal retrieval success indicates low trace stability, requiring shorter
subsequent intervals to prevent memory failure.
Because SM-2 freezes the ease factor at 2.50 when q = 4, the engine treats marginal
performance as evidence of standard memory durability, aggressively multiplying intervals by
250% on every review. A learner who barely succeeds is propelled from a fifteen-day interval
directly to thirty-eight and ninety-five days, ensuring an eventual catastrophic lapse. The ease

Diagnostic Credit Attribution via Q-Matrix Decomposition
In the current machine, flashcards derived from exam questions deliberately write back to
nothing: because exam items are not linked to specific chunks, grading them produces zero
diagnostic updates to sub-topic weakness records. Consequently, five out of eight cards in every
review deck produce no granular diagnostic signal.
This design discards valuable assessment data. In Cognitive Diagnostic Assessment (CDA),
psychometricians resolve attribution across multi-attribute items using a incidence matrix known
as a Q-matrix. The Q-matrix is an incident boolean mapping of dimensions J \times K, where J
denotes the number of assessment items and K denotes the latent attribute chunks:
Q = \begin{bmatrix} q_{jk} \end{bmatrix}, \quad q_{jk} \in \{0, 1\}
Because Knell’s curricular topology averages exactly 2.95 chunks per topic and five exam
questions per topic, authors can easily define a simple incidence mapping for each topic during
content creation:

When an examinee fails an exam-derived flashcard during a review, the penalty is
back-propagated to the topic’s latent chunk weakness vector \mathbf{W} = [w_1, w_2, \dots,
w_K] using normalized fractional weighting:

w_k \leftarrow w_k + \left( \frac{q_{jk}}{\sum_{m=1}^{K} q_{jm}} \right) \cdot \lambda
where \lambda represents an empirical weakness increment (e.g., \lambda = 0.25).
Under this model, failing an integrative flashcard that links Chunk 2 and Chunk 3 distributes an
equal 0.125 weakness increment to both chunks. If a subsequent review reveals a failure on an
atomic Chunk 2 card, that chunk crosses a diagnostic alert threshold, automatically scheduling
targeted remedial reviews without requiring manual diagnostic re-authoring.
